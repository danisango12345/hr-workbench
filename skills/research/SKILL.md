# Research

Gathers what's actually true about a question and writes it up, with every claim traced to where
it came from.

## The one rule

**Write down what's true — not what you think should happen.**

Research records findings. It does not recommend, judge, or solve. The moment you write "they
should" or "the risk here is", you've stopped researching and started deciding — and deciding
happens afterwards, with the findings in front of you.

This separation is what makes the eventual recommendation defensible. If the facts and the
conclusion are written in the same breath, nobody can later tell which drove which.

**Keep the chat quiet.** The document is the deliverable. Don't narrate the steps or read the
findings back — finish with one line saying it's done.

## When to use this

Good fits: a policy or handbook review, a compliance question, a comp or benchmark exercise,
"what do comparable organisations actually do", due diligence on existing practice, understanding
a team's current structure before redesigning it.

Poor fits: a question answerable in one line, or one where the answer is already known and only
the writing is wanted. This has real overhead and only pays off when the answer matters and has
to hold up.

## Before starting

Ask for whatever's missing:

1. The actual question
2. **Jurisdiction** — which country, state, or province. An employment-law finding without a
   jurisdiction is not a finding.
3. **Population** — who it applies to. All employees? One team? A specific level or contract type?
4. Any documents already to hand
5. Where the write-up should go

Don't guess at 2 or 3. Getting them wrong invalidates everything downstream, and they're one
question to settle.

## Step 1 — Read what's already there

Read anything named, in full. Read any existing research on the question, and anything already in
the project folder. If prior work exists on this topic, read that too — re-deriving a conclusion
someone already reached is the most common waste in advisory work.

**Don't move on until all of it has been read.** Everything after this depends on knowing what's
already known.

## Step 2 — Break the question into lines of enquiry

Think carefully about what would actually have to be true, and what evidence would settle it.

Most HR and operations questions decompose along some subset of these five. Use the ones that
apply — not all five, every time.

**What the organisation's own documents say.** Handbooks, contracts, policies, offer letters, job
architecture, org charts, prior agreements. This is where the answer usually already lives, and
where the contradictions hide.

**What the law requires.** Statute and regulation for the stated jurisdiction, as at today's
date. Note where a rule is about to change — a finding that's correct today and wrong in April is
a trap.

**What the market does.** Benchmark data, published surveys, what comparable organisations of
similar size and sector actually practise. Be specific about the comparator set; "market practice"
without a defined comparator group means nothing.

**What the internal numbers show.** Headcount, attrition, time-to-fill, distribution, cost.
Whatever the system of record actually says, as against what people believe it says.

**What's been concluded before.** Prior research, previous work, decisions already made and the
reasoning behind them.

## Step 3 — Investigate

Run the lines of enquiry in parallel where they don't depend on each other. Give each
investigation a narrow brief and this instruction: **report what the source says, quote it, cite
it, and stop there.** No interpretation, no recommendations.

If it's unclear where the answer even lives, do one quick scoping pass first before committing
effort to a full investigation. Aiming beats fanning out blindly.

**Wait for all of them to finish before writing anything.** A synthesis built on three of five
findings will be quietly wrong in ways that are hard to spot later.

## Step 4 — Grade every source

This is the step that makes HR research different from general research. Not all sources carry
the same weight, and a finding is only as strong as what it rests on.

| Tier | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A — Primary** | The thing itself | Statute, regulation, signed contracts and policies, system-of-record data |
| **B — Official** | Authoritative interpretation | Regulator or government guidance, established professional bodies, audited survey data |
| **C — Reputable** | Informed secondary | Law-firm briefings, established consultancies, peer-reviewed research |
| **D — Indicative** | Suggestive only | Vendor content, blogs, forums, single anecdotes, unattributed benchmarks |

Every claim carries its tier. The rule that matters: **nothing consequential rests on C or D
alone.** If a compliance finding or a comp decision is only supported at tier C, that isn't a
finding — it's a gap, and it goes in the gaps section.

## Step 5 — Separate what was found from what was concluded

A hard line between what a source actually says and what has been inferred from it. Inference
presented as finding is the dangerous failure — it survives review, because it reads exactly like
evidence.

Every inference is labelled as one, and says what it rests on.

## Step 6 — Write it up

Every claim needs a source, a locator, and a date. "The handbook says X" is not citable.
"Handbook §4.2, version dated March 2026" is.

Before writing, check three things:

- No placeholders. No "[to be confirmed]" sitting in a finished document.
- No invented specifics. If the number isn't known, say so. A plausible fabricated figure is
  worse than an acknowledged gap, because it survives review.
- No recommendations. If a sentence tells someone what to do, cut it — it belongs in the decision
  that follows, not here.

## What the write-up contains

```
# Research: [Question]

Date · Jurisdiction · Applies to [population]

## Question
[What was actually asked]

## Summary
[Two or three paragraphs answering the question with what was found. Findings only.
If the honest answer is "the documents contradict each other", say that.]

## Findings

### [Area]

**What the source says**: [Finding, stated plainly]

> [Direct quote where the exact wording matters — in compliance work it usually does]

**Source**: [Document or publication], [section or page], [date] — **Tier [A/B/C/D]**

**Inferred from this**: [Anything concluded rather than stated, clearly labelled, with
what it rests on. Omit if nothing was inferred.]

**How this connects**: [Relationship to other findings — contradictions, dependencies,
overlaps. Not implications for what to do.]

## Where sources disagree
[Two documents saying different things is a finding in its own right, and often the most
useful thing in the report. Don't resolve it here — record it.]

## As-at dates and expiry
[Anything time-sensitive. Statutory figures that change annually, benchmark data with a
vintage, a policy under review. State when each finding stops being reliable.]

## Gaps
- **Not available** — the data doesn't exist or wasn't accessible
- **Not sufficient** — only supported at tier C or D, needs a primary source
- **Needs a professional** — requires legal or tax advice rather than research

## Sources
| Source | Locator | Date | Tier |

## Open questions
[What must be answered before decisions can be made. One line each, with what it blocks.]
```

## Rules

**Stay inside the professional line.** Research reports what the law says. It does not advise on
whether an organisation is compliant, or what to do about a specific situation. Where a question
genuinely needs a lawyer, that goes in Gaps as "needs a professional" — clearly, not buried. That
flag protects the organisation and the person doing the research.

**Never decide about a named individual.** Research describes what's true in general. It does not
select, rank, or score specific people. Anything that would determine an outcome for a named
individual — redundancy selection, performance ranking, promotion shortlists, candidate screening
— is out of scope entirely, regardless of how the request is framed.

**Data handling.**

Never pull in or write down:

- Health, medical, disability, accommodations, or sick-leave reasons
- Anything from an active grievance or investigation

Individual pay and individual performance history are legitimate for some work — coaching
preparation needs performance history, a pay decision needs a pay rate — but **warn before
surfacing them**, so handling sensitive data is a conscious act rather than something that happens
incidentally while assembling context.

Aggregate by default. Never carry an individual's details into a document that will travel further
than the source did.

**Date everything.** Employment law, statutory rates, and benchmark data all have a shelf life. An
undated finding will eventually be read as current when it isn't.

**Follow-ups append.** A further question adds a dated follow-up section to the existing research.
Don't start a second document — the point is one place where everything known about this question
lives.

## When done

One line: what was researched, how many findings, how many sources.
