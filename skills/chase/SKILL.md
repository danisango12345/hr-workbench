# chase

Keeps track of what people committed to, surfaces it at the moment you can act on it, and helps
you hold the line when you'd otherwise let it slide.

**Also triggered by:** "herd cats", "herding cats", "I need help herding cats".

**It assembles the facts and prompts your thinking. You decide what to say.** No drafted messages,
no sent messages.

## The problem this solves

Three things, in order of difficulty:

1. **Remembering you were waiting at all.** Commitments aren't unrecorded — they're scattered
   across a project tool, your notes, Slack threads and meeting notes, and nothing pulls them
   together.
2. **Knowing when to chase.** Too early is nagging, too late and the deadline's gone.
3. **Holding firm.** Knowing someone is overloaded makes you quietly renegotiate their commitment
   on their behalf, without them in the room. This is the hardest one, and a reminder alone won't
   touch it.

## Where commitments live

Sweep all of these. Something agreed in one place rarely gets recorded in the others.

- **A project tool with a due date** — already structured. Read it.
- **Your own notes** — including anything captured after a 1:1 or partnering conversation.
- **Slack and email threads** — "I'll get you that by Friday" buried in a thread about something
  else.
- **Shared meeting notes** — where verbal commitments get transcribed. Look for who agreed to what.

Track **both directions**: what they owe you, and what you owe them. A follow-up from someone who
owes you three things themselves lands differently.

## When something surfaces

**Surface on change, not on existence.** The failure mode is a daily list re-showing the same
unchanged item until it's ignored. If nothing about an item has changed, say nothing.

Four triggers:

### About to be due

A day or two ahead — early enough to nudge, late enough that nudging is reasonable. Nothing before
that.

**If something needs runway, surface earlier.** A task due Friday that takes three days needs
flagging on Tuesday, not Thursday. Don't demand an effort estimate on everything — that
bookkeeping won't survive a busy week. But if the person says an item will take a while, or it's
obviously substantial, work back from the due date rather than sitting on it.

### About to see that person

The best trigger. Before a 1:1 or a meeting with someone, surface whatever's outstanding between
you — so it gets raised in person rather than chased in writing.

This catches most things at the best possible moment. It should feed directly into meeting
preparation rather than arriving as a separate notification.

### It's been sitting a while

Overdue for some time with no follow-up from you. **This one is about your drift, not theirs** —
it fires because you haven't chased, not because they haven't delivered.

### It's gone overdue

The date passed and nothing arrived. Surfaces **once**, when it changes state.

This is a failure signal. If the first three triggers are working, it should rarely fire — the
thing should have been raised before the date passed. **When it fires often, that's a finding
about the earlier nudges, not about people being late.** Say so.

## How it reaches you

**Meeting preparation** — outstanding items appear when preparing to see the person. No separate
alert.

**A daily watch** — runs each morning and is **silent on most days**. It speaks only when
something crossed its trigger. A watch that always has content becomes a list you skim; one that's
usually quiet gets read when it isn't.

**On demand** — "what am I waiting on?" or "herd cats for me" gives the whole picture, ordered by
what matters rather than by date.

## Ordering: urgency is not the due date

When several things surface together, order by what actually needs attention, not by date.

**Due date** is when it's needed. **Urgency** is when you have to act, which is earlier for
anything substantial. **Impact** is what it blocks and who's waiting downstream.

A complex item due in five days can be more urgent than a simple one due tomorrow. Order
accordingly, and say why the order is what it is when it isn't obvious.

## Holding firm

When surfacing something to chase, three of these are facts to retrieve. The fourth is a question
to ask.

**1. The commitment exactly as made.** Their words, the date they gave, unchanged. Quote it and say
where it came from.

This is the guard against softening it on their behalf. You're holding them to what they said, not
to what you're now asking for.

**2. That they chose it.** They agreed to the date; it wasn't imposed. This is the reframe that
empathy erodes — knowing someone is busy makes their commitment feel like your imposition, and it
isn't.

**3. The pattern, where there is one.** How often this person's commitments have slipped, with
specifics. Conditions below.

**4. What the delay costs — ask, don't answer.**

Put these questions to the person and let them work it out:

- What does this cost?
- What does this block?
- What does this delay?
- What do we lose by not doing this?

**Do not answer these on their behalf.** Reasoning through the consequence is what converts a
follow-up from a favour being asked into a consequence being flagged — and it only works if
they've done the reasoning themselves. Handing them a ready-made case means walking into the
conversation with an argument they haven't thought through, which collapses the moment it's
questioned.

If they can't answer the questions, that's useful too. Something with no articulable cost may not
warrant chasing at all — or the cost is real but hasn't been surfaced yet, which is worth knowing
before raising it.

### The condition on the pattern

**It exists to be shared with the person, not held about them.**

A record of missed commitments used to give someone direct feedback is exactly what good
management requires — you can't tell someone their commitments slip without examples. The same
record kept quietly and never raised is a shadow file.

So: surface the pattern **when it's actionable as feedback**, and say plainly that this is feedback
material. If it's been surfaced repeatedly and never raised with the person, flag that — holding a
pattern you never share is the failure this rule guards against.

Facts only: what was agreed, what date, what happened. **No ratings, no reliability scores, no
comparisons between people.**

---

## What this doesn't do

**No drafted messages.** It assembles what's needed to make the case. Composing it is theirs —
tone, timing, and channel depend on the relationship.

**No sending.** Nothing goes out without them.

**No reasoning done for them.** Facts get retrieved; consequences get asked about.

**No judgments about people.** Facts about commitments, never assessments of the person.

## Notice avoidance

If items are being surfaced repeatedly and never raised with anyone, say so. Tracking is not
chasing, and a well-maintained list of things nobody has been told about is avoidance with good
record-keeping.
