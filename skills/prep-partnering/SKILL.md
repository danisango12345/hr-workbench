# prep-partnering

Prepares an HR professional for a conversation with a business leader they partner with, and
captures what came out of it afterwards.

**The notes are private to you.** This is your working record as a practitioner, not a shared
document. That distinction shapes everything below.

## Four steps

1. Establish what the conversation is and who it's with
2. Gather what's relevant from connected sources
3. Surface a few questions worth having ready
4. Capture afterwards — four questions, into your running notes

---

## Step 1: What kind of conversation?

Ask, don't assume. Each pulls different context and needs different preparation.

**A regular partnering check-in** — recurring rhythm with a leader. Continuity matters most: what
changed since last time, what's outstanding.

**A problem they've raised** — they've come to you with something. Understanding the problem
properly matters more than a broad agenda.

**Something you need to raise** — you're initiating. Preparation is about anticipating resistance
and what buy-in would take, not what to ask.

**A planning conversation** — headcount, comp cycle, restructure, workforce planning. Data-heavy.
What the numbers say versus what they believe.

**A conversation about a specific person on their team** — see the data rules below. Handled
carefully.

**A new partnership** — you haven't worked with this leader before. Preparation is about
understanding their world, not about an agenda.

Also establish **who they are**: their function, what their team does, how long you've partnered,
and whether this is recurring or a one-off. One-offs don't need a running document; recurring
conversations do.

---

## Step 2: Gather what's relevant

Pull from what's connected — Drive, Notion, email, calendar. What matters depends on the
conversation type.

**Always:**

- **Your previous notes on this leader.** What did they tell you last time? What did you agree to?
  Has it happened?
- **The designed alliance between you and them**, if one exists — the working agreement you built
  together covering how you communicate, how they like feedback, what moves them from resistance to
  momentum, how you'll know if you've annoyed them. Surface it with a link so it can be reopened.
  It's the most useful single document for a partnering conversation and the one most often
  forgotten. If none exists, say so — that's worth knowing before a difficult conversation.
- Anything outstanding in either direction

**For a regular check-in:** what's changed in their team since you last spoke — joiners, leavers,
role changes, anything you've been working on that affects them.

**For a problem they've raised:** what you already know about it. Whether it's come up before, with
them or elsewhere. Whether there's policy or precedent.

**For something you need to raise:** their stated priorities, anything they've pushed back on
before, and what's already on their plate. Resistance is usually about timing and bandwidth rather
than the idea.

**For planning:**

- The actual numbers — headcount, open roles, attrition, cost. What the system of record says, as
  distinct from what people believe it says.
- **Company and department OKRs**, where relevant. A planning conversation that isn't anchored to
  what the business said it's trying to achieve is a wishlist. Pull both levels: the company
  objectives, and this leader's own — then check whether the plan being discussed actually serves
  them, or quietly serves something else. Where the two levels don't line up, that's a finding
  worth raising.

**For a new partnership:** their function, team size and shape, recent history, and anything
previous partners recorded.

**Report what you couldn't find**, and what would need a person to answer. A gap you know about is
useful; a gap you don't is dangerous.

### Data rules

**Never gather or write down:** health, medical, disability, accommodations, or sick-leave reasons;
anything from a live grievance or investigation.

Recognising these takes judgment, not a keyword check — "adjusted hours during treatment" is health
information even though it names no condition.

**Individual pay and performance history:** legitimately relevant to some partnering conversations
— a promotion case, a retention risk, a comp discussion. **Say so before surfacing it**, so
handling it is a deliberate choice rather than something that happens incidentally.

**Never rank, score, or select named people.** Partnering conversations often circle a decision
about an individual. Assemble the context; don't produce the judgment. That includes flight-risk
scoring, readiness ratings, and comparative assessments between team members.

## Step 3: Questions worth having ready

Two source documents in Drive. Draw from whichever fits the conversation.

**People Partnering Question Bank** — business and priorities · understanding the leader · team
effectiveness · talent and retention · organisational health · looking around corners.

**Coaching and feedback questions** — a wider set, and better matched to specific situations:

| Conversation | Pull from |
| --- | --- |
| Regular check-in | Question Bank, any category · *People Partnering: sparking conversation* |
| A problem they've raised | *Creating awareness* — what's the current situation, what have you tried, what's getting in the way · then *Action plans and accountabilities* |
| Something you need to raise | *Initiatives and projects* — what will you push back on, what would it take to get your buy-in, what context does this fail to consider |
| Planning | *Strategic workforce planning* · *OKRs* · *Painting done* |
| A follow-up | *Follow-up conversation questions* — how's the plan going, what's working, what have you learned |
| A new partnership | Question Bank: understanding the leader, understanding the business |

**Four rules for this step:**

**Surface, don't invent.** Questions come from the documents. If something not in them seems worth
asking, say clearly that it's a suggestion rather than presenting it as though it came from the
bank.

**Three or four, never more.** A long list becomes a script, and a script produces an interrogation
rather than a conversation.

**Ask which they'll actually use, and why.** This is the point of the step. Choosing between
questions is where the thinking happens — handing over a list to work through skips it.

**Don't script the conversation.** Questions are scaffolding; the answers come from the leader and
have to be responded to live. Don't draft what to say, how to open, or how to respond to a likely
answer. If asked for words, don't supply them.

---

## Step 4: Capture

For recurring or follow-up conversations, this is what the next preparation reads. Without it, prep
has nothing and the loop doesn't exist.

**First ask whether they already have notes.** If yes, structure those and only ask what's missing.
If no, run the four questions. Same output either way.

### The four questions

1. **What did you learn that you didn't know before?**
2. **What did you agree to? What did they agree to?**
3. **Did you push back on anything, or advise against something? What was your reasoning?**
4. **What are you keeping an eye on?**

**On the third:** this is the one people skip and the one that matters most later. When a leader
wants something carrying legal risk, cutting against policy, or simply a bad idea, the record that
you advised against it — and why — is your professional protection. It's also how you learn, over
time, whether your advice lands.

**On the fourth:** early signals, not problems yet. The value shows up months later when something
you flagged becomes real and you already had it in view.

### Where it goes

For recurring conversations, a running document per leader:

```
[Your name] | [Leader name] partnering
```

Newest entry at the top, dated. Private to you.

For a one-off, ask whether it's worth keeping. Sometimes it isn't.

### What feeds the follow-up

The commitments from question 2 are exactly what a chase or follow-up process tracks — both what
you owe them and what they owe you. Capture isn't a dead end.

---

## What this skill doesn't do

**No shared documents.** These notes are yours. If something needs to be visible to the leader,
that's a separate document you create deliberately, not this one.

**No drafting what you'll say.** Preparation only.

## Notice avoidance

If the request suggests the tool is being used to sidestep a conversation rather than prepare for
one — wanting a concern recorded but not raised, asking for wording that softens a point past
recognition, a difficult item appearing repeatedly with no sign it was ever said aloud — say so
plainly.

A record that a concern existed is not the same as having raised it. A private note saying you were
worried is worth nothing if you never told them.
