# 1-on-1

A loop with four moments. Two happen on their own; two need you.

| When | What | Automatic? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business day before | Nudge both people to add agenda items | Yes, if Slack is connected |
| Morning of | Assemble the agenda into the shared document | Yes |
| Before the meeting | Your own preparation — how you'll handle it | No, and never written down |
| After the meeting | Capture what was agreed | No |

Each prep is only useful because the last capture happened. That's the loop, and it's the point.

Works for your own 1:1s, and designed to be handed to a manager who hasn't been coached in how to
run one.

## What goes in the shared document — and what never does

**In the document:** the agenda, and afterwards what was agreed.

**Never in the document:** anything from the manager's own preparation. The opener they're planning,
their answer to "how will you handle this", their thinking about a difficult item, any coaching
prompt and its response.

This matters. A report opening the shared doc should see the topics and what was agreed — not their
manager's notes on how to broach something with them. That's worse than not writing it at all.

The manager's preparation stays in the conversation where it happened. If they want to keep it, that
is theirs, somewhere private.

## Setting up a pairing

Two things exist per pairing, both named by convention so they can be found without being told where
they are each time.

**The shared document** — in Google Drive or Notion, named:

```
[Name] | [Name] 1:1s
```

One running document per pairing, and **both people must be able to open it.** This is a
requirement, not a preference: without it there's nothing to prepare from and nothing to close the
loop against.

**The calendar event** — in Google Calendar, named:

```
[Name] | [Name]
```

Recurring, same time and day each cycle. This is where the meeting date comes from, which is what
makes the timing of everything else work.

**Get approval once per pairing** before automating anything into a document a colleague can see, or
sending them a recurring message. It's a standing arrangement and should be agreed knowingly, per
person.

## Finding them

Given only a person's name — "prep me for my 1:1 with Sarah" — work it out rather than asking:

1. **Search the calendar** for a recurring event with both names. That gives the next occurrence.
2. **Search Drive and Notion** for a document with both names and "1:1" in the title.
3. **Confirm what you found** before using it — name the document and the meeting time back, so a
   wrong match gets caught before anything is written into the wrong person's record.

**Match on the names, not their order.** "Dave | Dani 1:1s" and "Dani | Dave" are the same pairing.

**If the document isn't found**, it probably doesn't exist yet. Offer to create it, shared with both
people from the start, rather than asking for a link.

**If the calendar event isn't found**, ask when the 1:1 is and whether it recurs. Don't guess a date.

## The business day before: nudge

An agenda nobody is reminded to fill is an agenda nobody fills.

**First, check whether Slack is connected.**

- **Not connected** — skip this step entirely. Don't mention it, don't offer workarounds, don't ask
  the person to send it manually. The rest of the loop works without it.
- **Connected** — check what it permits. If it can't post messages, treat it as not connected.

**When it can run:** on the morning of the **business day before** the 1:1, send both people the link
to the shared document and a reminder to add their items.

Business day, not calendar day — a working day to think, not a weekend notification:

- 1:1 on Tuesday → nudge Monday morning
- 1:1 on Monday → nudge Friday morning
- 1:1 on Tuesday after a public holiday Monday → nudge Friday morning

Keep it plain and identical every time. A reminder, not a communication:

> 1:1 tomorrow — add anything you want to cover: [link]

Both parties get it. **Skip the cycle if both have already added items** — a reminder to do something
already done trains people to ignore reminders. No topics, no content, just the link and the ask.

## The morning of: assemble the agenda

This runs on its own. Set up one scheduled task that runs each weekday morning, checks the calendar
for 1:1s happening that day, and assembles the agenda for each into its shared document. Not a task
per pairing — one task that looks at the day.

By the time either person opens the document, the agenda is there.

Build it in this order. **The order matters more than the content** — whatever sits at the top is
what fills the half hour.

### 1. Their agenda first

The report's items come first, always. Best-evidenced finding about 1:1s: both people rate the
meeting higher when the report sets the agenda.

If they haven't added anything, note that plainly rather than filling the space. A report who never
brings items either isn't being asked to, doesn't believe it's theirs, or is disengaged. Filling the
gap hides it.

### 2. From last time — and did it happen

Pull what was agreed last time and list each item as a question, not a recap: **"we said X — did
it?"**

Anything that hasn't moved after two or three cycles gets flagged rather than quietly rolled forward
again.

### 3. Blockers

What's in their way that they can't move themselves. Often the highest-value use of the time.

### 4. Goals and development

Progress against what they're working toward, and development that isn't about delivery.

If development hasn't been discussed for months, say so — that's the item.

### 5. Their manager's items

Last. Including anything developmental — see below.

### Not on the agenda: status

**No status or progress section.** Over half of managers say the purpose of a 1:1 *is* a status
update, and a status slot on the page guarantees it fills the meeting.

Status belongs in a written update, a project tool, or a stand-up. This meeting is for what can't go
in a spreadsheet. If a project genuinely needs discussing, it appears as a blocker or a decision.

## Raising something difficult

**In the document: the reframe.** Written as an area for development, followed by the word `context`
as a marker only — never the detail.

> Area of development: even tone during calls — context

The topic is on the table so nobody is ambushed and they can think beforehand. The evidence isn't in
writing, where it becomes a cold accusation read alone.

**In the room: direct.** The specifics, with examples, from someone who can read the reaction.

## Before the meeting: the manager's own preparation

Separate from the agenda, and **none of this goes in the shared document.**

When there's a difficult item, prompt: *"how are you going to open this one?"*

Do not draft the opening line, the framing, or the words. They write it. Thinking it through is how
they get better at hard conversations, and a manager reading out a supplied script learns nothing and
sounds like it. This holds even when they ask for the words. Especially then.

Other prompts that belong here, not in the document:

- What have they already tried on that blocker?
- When did you last talk to them about something other than work?
- What would you want to hear if you were them?

Answers stay in the conversation. If the manager wants a record, that's theirs to keep privately.

## Notice avoidance

If someone is using this to avoid a conversation rather than prepare for one, say so.

Signs: wanting a concern recorded but not raised; asking for wording that softens the point past
recognition; a development item appearing three times with no sign it was ever said aloud; asking for
a written version of something that should be said in person.

Point them to their HR partner. A tool that lets someone discharge a duty of care by writing it down
has done harm, not work.

## After: capture

Short, into the shared document under a dated heading. Not automatic — this needs the person, soon
after, while they remember.

**What was agreed** — who's doing it, by when. This is what the next agenda depends on.

**Decisions made** — so they aren't relitigated in three weeks.

**That a concern was raised** — where a development item was discussed, record *that it happened*
without the raw detail:

> Discussed tone on customer calls, gave recent examples, agreed to revisit in two weeks

If the reframe is all that's ever written, the file six months later shows a development heading and
nothing showing the concern was raised, with examples, on a date. That record is what makes later
action defensible — and it's fair to the report, who should be able to see the conversation happened.

**Don't record** raw evidence, private assessments, the manager's preparation, or anything not said
out loud. If it isn't fit to be seen by the person it's about, it doesn't belong there — and if it's
true and important, it should have been said.

## Data handling

**Never write in:** health, medical, disability, accommodations, or sick leave reasons; anything from
a live grievance or investigation.

Recognising these takes judgment, not a keyword check. "Adjusted hours during treatment" is health
information even though it names no condition.

**Individual pay and performance history** can be legitimately relevant — a promotion conversation
needs both. Say so before surfacing them, so it's deliberate rather than incidental.

## The first one

With a new report or a new document there's no history. Don't invent one.

Set up the document, confirm the calendar event, then use the first meeting to establish what the 1:1
is for, how often, who owns the agenda, and what they want from it. Record that — it becomes the
"from last time" for the second meeting.

## Why the order is what it is

Most 1:1s become status meetings, and it isn't laziness. Status is easier to talk about than a
person: it fills time, feels productive, and asks nothing of either party.

The order defends against that. Their items first because it's their meeting. Last time's commitments
second because unclosed loops are how trust erodes. Blockers third because clearing them is the
manager's actual job. Status nowhere, because it will take the whole meeting if it's allowed on the
page.
